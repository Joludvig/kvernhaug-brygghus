A general walkthrough of a typical all-grain brew day, step by step. The exact order and some steps vary with brewing method and equipment — this is the typical sequence most homebrewers follow.

1Prep Equipment and Ingredients

What do I do?
Weigh out malt and hops according to the recipe, clean and set out the kettle, mash tun, chiller, fermenter, and everything else you'll need along the way.
Why do I do it?
Hunting for equipment or missing an ingredient mid-mash or mid-boil costs you precious time exactly when temperature and timing matter most.
What should I watch for?
That everything that needs to be clean actually is clean — especially anything that touches the wort after the boil.
Common beginner mistakes
Discovering after mashing has started that you're missing an ingredient, or that the chiller isn't hooked up to water.

2Heat the Mash Water

What do I do?
Heat the amount of water the mash needs to a few degrees above your target temperature (cold grain and cold equipment pull the temperature down when the water meets the malt).
Why do I do it?
Mash temperature determines which type of sugar the enzymes produce — and therefore how fermentable the wort becomes. See mashing.
What should I watch for?
That you hit the right temperature after the malt has been added, not just in the water alone.
Common beginner mistakes
Heating the water to exactly the target temperature, then missing because cold grain pulls the temperature down a couple of degrees.

3Add the Malt

What do I do?
Stir the crushed malt into the water, evenly and without clumps ("dough-in").
Why do I do it?
Clumps of dry malt ("dough balls") stop the enzymes from reaching the whole grain, and you lose yield.
What should I watch for?
Stir thoroughly right away, especially at the bottom and along the edges.
Common beginner mistakes
Pouring in all the malt at once without stirring at the same time.

4Mash

What do I do?
Hold the mixture at the correct temperature for the time the recipe specifies (typically 45–90 minutes), see mashing.
Why do I do it?
This is when the enzymes actually convert starch into fermentable sugar — the foundation of the whole batch.
What should I watch for?
Temperature drift. Insulation (lid, blanket) helps a lot on simple mash tuns.
Common beginner mistakes
Letting the temperature drop gradually without noticing.

5Mash-out (If Relevant)

What do I do?
Quickly heat the mash to around 75–78°C, see mash-out.
Why do I do it?
Stops enzyme activity (locking in the sugar profile) and makes the wort less viscous, which makes sparging easier.
What should I watch for?
Don't let the temperature go too high or hold too long — the point is to stop the process, not boil the mash.
Common beginner mistakes
Skipping mash-out and then wondering why sparging is slow.

6Sparge (If the Method Uses It)

What do I do?
Rinse remaining sugar out of the grain bed with hot water, see sparging and brewing methods for which methods use this step.
Why do I do it?
Without sparging, a lot of the malt potential is left behind in the grain bed — it directly affects OG.
What should I watch for?
The sparge water temperature (too hot can extract unwanted tannins).
Common beginner mistakes
Sparging with boiling water, or squeezing the grain bed hard to "get more out" — both can produce bitter byproducts.

7Boil

What do I do?
Boil the wort, normally for 60 minutes, see boil.
Why do I do it?
Sterilization, concentrating the wort, and the foundation for the hop additions below.
What should I watch for?
A vigorous boil is prone to boiling over in the first few minutes — stay nearby until the boil has settled.
Common beginner mistakes
Walking away from the kettle right as the boil starts and coming back to a boiled-over stovetop.

8Hop Additions

What do I do?
Add hops at the times the recipe specifies, see hop additions.
Why do I do it?
Timing determines whether the hops contribute mostly bitterness (early) or aroma/flavor (late), see IBU.
What should I watch for?
Actually timing the additions against remaining boil time, not elapsed time.
Common beginner mistakes
Mixing up "minutes into the boil" and "minutes left in the boil" — they count in opposite directions.

9Chill

What do I do?
Rapidly chill the wort down toward the yeast's starting temperature, see chilling.
Why do I do it?
Rapid chilling reduces the time the wort spends in a temperature range where unwanted bacteria thrive, and often gives clearer beer.
What should I watch for?
That your chilling equipment is actually clean — it meets the wort after the boil, the most vulnerable phase.
Common beginner mistakes
Letting the wort sit and cool slowly at room temperature instead of using a chiller/ice bath.

10Take an OG Reading

What do I do?
Measure the gravity with a hydrometer or refractometer, see OG reading.
Why do I do it?
Confirms whether you hit your expected OG — an important starting point for assessing the whole batch.
What should I watch for?
That the sample has cooled to the correct reference temperature for your instrument.
Common beginner mistakes
Measuring a warm sample and getting a misleading number, see the FAQ.

11Transfer to the Fermenter

What do I do?
Transfer the chilled wort to a clean, sanitized fermenter.
Why do I do it?
From this point on, it's the yeast that should dominate the environment — not other microorganisms.
What should I watch for?
A bit of splashing during transfer is actually desirable here — the wort needs oxygen for the yeast to get off to a good start.
Common beginner mistakes
Transferring too gently and giving the yeast too little oxygen for a good start.

12Pitch Yeast

What do I do?
Add the yeast (rehydrated or pitched directly, per the manufacturer's instructions).
Why do I do it?
The faster the yeast gets going, the less time unwanted microorganisms have to establish themselves first.
What should I watch for?
That the wort is within the yeast's recommended temperature range before you pitch.
Common beginner mistakes
Pitching yeast into wort that's too warm, which can damage or kill the yeast.

13Fermentation

What do I do?
Let the yeast work undisturbed, at a stable temperature, see fermentation.
Why do I do it?
A stable temperature gives a cleaner, more predictable flavor profile than a fluctuating one.
What should I watch for?
Activity in the airlock over the first few days, and that the temperature stays within the yeast's recommended range.
Common beginner mistakes
Opening the fermenter often "to check" — every opening is a new chance for contamination.

14Take an FG Reading

What do I do?
Measure the gravity once activity has settled down, see FG reading.
Why do I do it?
Confirms that fermentation is actually finished, and lets you calculate the actual ABV.
What should I watch for?
Take readings on two consecutive days — if the number is stable, fermentation is done.
Common beginner mistakes
Ending fermentation too early because the airlock has gone quiet, without confirming with an actual reading.

15Carbonate / Bottle / Keg

What do I do?
Transfer the beer to bottles or a keg and carbonate it, see carbonation and bottling/kegging.
Why do I do it?
This is the last step before the beer is ready to drink — and what gives it the right mouthfeel.
What should I watch for?
The correct sugar amount for bottle carbonation (too much can cause overpressure/explosion risk), or the correct pressure/time for force-carbonating in a keg.
Common beginner mistakes
Drinking the beer too soon — most beers improve noticeably after a few weeks of conditioning.
