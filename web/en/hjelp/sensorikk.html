This page is a Master Brewer extension of the troubleshooting FAQ in Help & FAQ, which already covers individual questions about sulfur, fusel, diacetyl and oxidation. Here we go further into a more systematic walkthrough of sensory evaluation and common off-flavours — without building a full chemical aroma database, an automatic diagnosis tool, or a sensory scoring system.

How to taste systematically

Use five simple steps every time: look, smell, taste, mouthfeel, aftertaste. Always smell before you taste — the short, calm sniffs often tell you more than the first big gulp.

Look: clarity, colour, head, visible particles.

Smell: short, calm sniffs. Assess malt, hop and yeast character first — look for deviations afterward.

Taste: sweetness, bitterness, sourness, malt, hops, yeast character, alcohol.

Mouthfeel: body, carbonation, warmth, astringency, creamy or dry.

Aftertaste: what lingers? Bitterness, alcohol, sweetness, metallic?

Don't start by hunting for a flaw. Start by describing what's actually there — a diagnosis, if any, comes afterward, not first.

Normal style character vs. off-flavour

Many flavours and aromas can be correct in one beer style and unwanted in another. Esters, phenols, sulfur, alcohol warmth, and even certain oxidation notes in aged strong beers can all be part of a deliberate, desired profile — depending on what beer it actually is.

Avoid "this flavour is always wrong". Ask instead: is this expected for this particular style and this particular beer, or not?

Acetaldehyde

Typical sensory impression: green apple, raw apple skin, a fresh or unripe character.

Possible causes include the beer being packaged or chilled too early, incomplete fermentation, stressed yeast, and in some contexts oxidation can contribute to it forming again.

The character can often be reduced if the yeast gets time to clean up on its own — don't cold crash or package before FG is stable. Green apple still doesn't automatically mean acetaldehyde without the rest of the context.

DMS (dimethyl sulfide)

Typical descriptions: cooked corn, canned corn, vegetal — some perceive a cabbage-like note.

DMS precursors occur naturally in malt and are volatile during the boil. Insufficient evaporation during the boil and slow cooling afterward can, in some processes, contribute to increasing the risk.

Don't oversimplify to "always boil 90 minutes" — malt, process and system affect the risk differently, and some malt types contribute more than others.

Diacetyl

Sensory: butter, butterscotch, and in some cases a smooth or oily mouthfeel.

Builds on diacetyl and diacetyl rest. Possible causes: the yeast hasn't finished cleaning up, fermentation was interrupted or chilled too early, some yeast strains produce more than others, and contamination can also cause diacetyl.

Don't assume diacetyl always just needs more time — if infection is actually the cause, ageing alone won't help. See infection below.

Sulfur

Sensory: struck match, egg, sulfurous — at strong levels it can sometimes come across as sewage-like.

See also sulfur in the FAQ. Certain lager yeasts and other strains noticeably produce sulfur during fermentation, and much of it can dissipate with further fermentation and conditioning — it's not automatically a sign of infection.

For a persistent, strong deviation long after conditioning, consider yeast health, temperature, nutrients and contamination — but not before you've given it some time first.

Fusel / higher alcohols

Sensory: sharp alcohol, solvent-like, hot or burning. A "nail polish" association can occur, but don't lump all solvent notes together without nuance.

Possible factors: high fermentation temperature, stressed yeast, high OG, insufficient pitch rate, and oxygenation/nutrients can play a role — see yeast health and temperature and strong beer / high gravity.

Not all alcohol warmth in strong beers is fusel — some can mellow with conditioning. See fusel in the FAQ.

Esters

Typical fruity notes: banana, pear, apple, stone fruit, tropical fruit.

The amount is influenced by yeast strain, fermentation temperature, pitch rate and process overall. See esters in yeast selection for how to steer this through strain and process.

Don't present "esters = flaw" — in many styles this is exactly the desired character, not a deviation.

Phenols

Clove, pepper, spice — smoke, plastic or medicinal character can occur depending on the source.

Distinguish between desired yeast phenols in styles such as saison, weizen and Belgian beers, and an unwanted plastic or medicinal character. Possible unwanted sources can include contamination, chlorine-/chloramine-related compounds, or simply the wrong yeast character for the style.

Don't learn that all clove is a flaw — the context (the style and the rest of the impression) decides.

Oxidation

Builds on oxidation after fermentation. Sensory can be cardboard, stale notes, muted hop aroma, darker colour, or a sherry-/dried-fruit character in some aged strong beers.

Some oxidation notes can be perceived positively in aged strong beers, but oxidation is still not a goal in itself. Avoid unnecessary air contact during transfers and storage.

Astringency

Builds on tannins and astringency. Sensory: dry, rough, puckering, a "tea bag" feeling — gums and cheeks can feel tight.

Clearly distinguish this from bitterness and from a low-FG/dry taste — three different things that are easily mixed up.

Metallic

Typical: iron, blood, coins, a metallic aftertaste.

Possible sources: water, metal contact in the equipment, ingredients — but some sensory impressions can also simply be misread as metallic.

Don't automatically point to rust without actually inspecting the equipment first.

Sour / tart

Distinguish between planned sourness (sour beers and certain other styles) and unexpected sourness in a beer that isn't meant to be sour.

Unexpected sourness can be caused by infection, very low pH, or ingredient/process influence.

Don't learn that "sour = infected" without context — see also infection below.

Sweet / high FG

Builds on the FAQ on high FG. Sensory sweetness can come from residual sugar, dextrins, malt character, alcohol, low bitterness, or low carbonation affecting the perception.

High FG can be caused by low-fermentability wort, yeast stress, alcohol tolerance, temperature, or simply a measurement error.

Don't conflate sensory sweetness and FG as if they're the same thing — they can be related, but they aren't identical.

Too bitter / harsh

Distinguish between high IBU, a harsh or sharply perceived bitterness, astringency, a sulfate effect, and hop particles or polyphenols.

Why you care: The goal is that you don't automatically conclude "too much hops" — several of these sources feel quite similar but have completely different solutions. See hop troubleshooting for a more detailed rundown.

Flat / lifeless

Possible explanations: low carbonation, oxidation, poor balance, too high or too low serving temperature, or little aroma.

This isn't a full serving guide — just a reminder that "flat" rarely has a single cause.

Overcarbonated

See also carbonation in the FAQ. Possible causes: too much priming sugar, fermentation wasn't finished at bottling, uneven sugar distribution, contamination, or too much pressure with forced carbonation.

If you suspect bottles with dangerous overpressure, you should not be encouraged toward risky handling — see the safety warning in the brew day guide and handle with care.

Infection — don't diagnose from a single flavour

See also suspected infection in the FAQ. Infection can produce many different sensory expressions, and a single smell or taste is rarely enough for a reliable diagnosis on its own.

Consider several things together instead: appearance, smell, taste, pH, unexpected further attenuation, overcarbonation, and the process/hygiene history of this particular batch — see cleaning and sanitizing.

Avoid panic diagnoses from a single impression — gather several observations before you conclude.

Can it improve with age?

A nuanced, but not absolute, assessment. No guarantee — these are tendencies, not fixed rules.

Troubleshooting flow

A simple decision sequence when something seems off:

  1. Describe the sensory impression without diagnosing.
  2. Compare against the style/recipe.
  3. Check SG/FG and the process log.
  4. Check fermentation temperature.
  5. Check hygiene/transfers.
  6. Consider time/conditioning.
  7. Change only one thing for the next brew, if possible.

This fits KBH's own philosophy — observe, understand, adjust, learn — rather than jumping straight to a random fix.

Practical sensory log

A short, recommended log structure for each tasting: date, beer temperature, appearance, aroma, taste, mouthfeel, aftertaste, suspected deviation, possible process links, and change since the last tasting.

This page doesn't build its own logging feature yet. The existing Brew log page — which already covers what you planned, what actually happened, and what you'll carry into the next brew — could be a natural place to extend with sensory history later.

Sensory evaluation is about describing before you judge. Most deviations have more than one possible cause, and few should cause panic. Use the troubleshooting flow above, note what you actually perceive, and make one change at a time for the next brew.
