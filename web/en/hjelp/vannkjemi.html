This page is a Master Brewer extension of the short water chemistry introduction in Help & FAQ, which already covers pH, Ca, Mg, Na, Cl, SO4 and the Cl:SO4 ratio. Here we go further into what's often missing between "I know the ions" and "I understand why I'm actually adjusting the water" — without building a full chemistry textbook or new calculation engines in the app's "💧 Water Chemistry" module.

Alkalinity

Alkalinity describes water's ability to resist a change in pH — its buffering capacity, not the pH value itself. High alkalinity makes it harder for the malt and any acid to lower mash pH toward the desired range; low alkalinity gives the water less buffering to work against.

Don't mix up alkalinity and pH — they measure two different things. Two water samples can have roughly the same pH but very different alkalinity, and it's the alkalinity that determines how much resistance the malt/acid actually has to work against to move pH where you want it.

Bicarbonate (HCO3-)

Bicarbonate is often a major contributor to alkalinity in brewing water. High bicarbonate can counteract the pH drop malt normally causes in the mash, and how much is actually needed depends on both the malt profile and the rest of the water profile — not on bicarbonate alone.

Dark beer is historically often associated with more alkaline water, because dark/roasted malt itself contributes more acid to the mash — more alkalinity can in some cases balance out this acid. This is NOT a rule that "dark beer always needs lots of bicarbonate" — it depends on exactly how dark and how much roasted malt the recipe actually uses. Note also that alkalinity exists in three different, often-confused forms (HCO3 in mg/L, alkalinity "as CaCO3" in mg/L, and alkalinity in mmol/L) — the app's internal water calculation can convert between them, but the three figures aren't the same number and shouldn't be mixed without such a conversion.

Malt and Mash pH

Different malt types affect mash pH differently. Roughly speaking: pale base malt has a relatively small acid effect of its own, caramel/crystal malt sits somewhere in between, and dark/roasted malt usually contributes the most toward lowering pH on its own.

The same water can therefore behave quite differently in two different recipes — a pale pilsner and a dark stout with an identical water profile will often need a completely different acid/salt approach to hit the same mash-pH target. This is a general rule of thumb, not a malt-specific acid model.

Mash pH — What The Brewer Is Actually Looking For

Mash pH affects more than just enzyme activity: it's tied to extraction efficiency, flavour, clarity/process later in brewing, and how easily tannins are extracted from the grain (see tannins and astringency below).

Avoid hunting for "one magic number". KBH's own target profiles in the "💧 Water Chemistry" module use different mash-pH working ranges for different beer styles (typically somewhere between about 5.2 and 5.6, depending on the profile) — these are guiding working ranges, not universal correct answers. The app's own measurement form records whether the sample was cooled to room temperature before reading, precisely because a hot mash sample normally gives a different pH reading than a cooled sample of the exact same wort — not because a pH meter with temperature compensation is "lying", but because the pH value itself can genuinely shift with temperature. So never conflate "the electrode is temperature-compensated" with "pH is identical at every temperature" — those are two different things. See measuring pH.

Acid Additions

Acid is used to lower mash pH, reduce the effect of high alkalinity in the water, and optionally treat sparge water so it doesn't sit at too high a pH against the grain bed. KBH's "💧 Water Chemistry" module supports registering lactic acid and phosphoric acid as separate acid additions, in addition to acid malt being a common alternative many brewers use instead of, or alongside, liquid acid.

No acid is universally best — they have different strengths and come in different concentrations (both lactic acid and phosphoric acid, for example, are sold in several different strengths), and too much acid can affect flavour. Dosing must be based on a combination of the water, the malt profile and the desired mash pH — the app therefore always asks you to enter the concentration (%) for each acid addition yourself, rather than assuming a fixed strength from the name alone. This page gives no universal mL doses.

Salts Are Not The Same As Acid

CaCl2 (calcium chloride) and CaSO4 (gypsum) are used primarily to adjust the ion profile — the flavour/process effect of Ca, Cl and SO4 — not as a substitute for acid.

Salts can affect water chemistry and therefore mash pH indirectly (particularly via calcium, see ion concentrations below), but they are not the same as adding acid. Avoid learning "more gypsum = lower pH, done" — the relationship is indirect and far from that simple. Baking soda (NaHCO3) is a good example of the opposite: it's a salt the app's salt database offers to RAISE HCO3/alkalinity, not to lower pH directly.

Chloride and Sulfate — Levels Before Ratios

Help &amp; FAQ shows that the Cl:SO4 ratio gives a rough signal of direction — more toward malt/fullness, or more toward a dry, sharp hop emphasis. That ratio alone says nothing about intensity, though:

Both of these water profiles have exactly the same Cl:SO4 ratio (0.5), but won't necessarily be perceived or behave the same way — Water B has ten times the actual concentration of both ions.

The ratio says something about direction, while the actual concentrations say something about intensity. Look at both figures in the app's expected end-profile table — not just the ratio. This page builds no flavour equation based on the ratio.

Calcium — Extended

Beyond what Help &amp; FAQ already mentions, calcium plays several roles: it's involved in the mash/water chemistry itself, in yeast health and flocculation, in later precipitation/clarification processes, and it can help lower pH indirectly together with phosphates naturally present in the malt.

More calcium isn't automatically better. The app's water module warns, for example, at low calcium — an internal, practical rule of thumb for brewing in this app, not an official drinking-water limit — but there's no equivalent "the higher the better" rule in the other direction. Aim for a sensible target profile, not a maximum figure.

Magnesium and Sodium

Magnesium is a nutrient yeast needs in small/moderate amounts, and malt normally already contributes some magnesium on its own. Sodium in moderate amounts can support a rounder, fuller mouthfeel.

High levels of both can become a problem, though: a lot of magnesium can give a harsh, bitter or mineral character, and a lot of sodium can become salty or sharp. The app's warnings use internal, practical rules of thumb for brewing (not official drinking-water limits) to flag unusually high levels of both — but these are warnings, not hard limits.

Sparge Water

As the mash is sparged, what comes out gradually gets weaker — both in sugar concentration and in buffering strength. High pH in the sparge water or in the grain bed itself toward the end of sparging increases the risk of unwanted tannin extraction, see tannins and astringency below.

Sparging a lot more can therefore give worse beer quality, even if you extract a little more sugar along the way — more yield isn't automatically worth the cost. See batch sparge and fly sparge for how the sparging method itself works in KBH.

Over-Sparging

Signs of over-sparging include steadily weaker runnings (what comes out gets thinner and thinner), lower sugar concentration in the runoff, and pH in the remaining grain bed/water possibly rising toward the end — which in turn can make extraction of polyphenols/tannins more likely.

Avoid turning this into a fixed rule like "X litres of sparging is always too much" — it depends on grain bill, water profile, pH, your system, and the efficiency actually achieved. Use the signs above as a practical pointer, not an exact cutoff.

Tannins and Astringency

Bitterness is a flavour — it typically comes from hops. Astringency is something else: a mouthfeel, often described as dry, puckering or "tea-bag-like", and can come from tannins/polyphenols extracted from the grain husk.

Possible contributors to astringency include high pH during sparging, over-sparging (see above), very hot handling of the grain under unfavourable pH conditions, and to some extent how the malt is milled/handled. Don't learn that "sparge water that's too hot alone" automatically causes tannins — pH and the rest of the process context matter at least as much as temperature alone. Polyphenols also contribute to haze in the finished beer, see clarity.

RO / Distilled Water

RO or distilled water has a very low mineral load — in practice it gives you a "blanker canvas" to build a water profile on, without unknown or unwanted contributions from the source water.

RO water isn't automatically "better water" — it also means desired ions often have to be built back up from scratch with salts, instead of adjusting a source water that already has something to build on. This page builds no RO-blending calculator of its own; use the app's normal source-water/target-profile fields no matter which starting water you choose.

Water Profile As A Whole

pH, alkalinity, Ca, Mg, Na, Cl and SO4 can't be adjusted completely independently of each other — a change to one can affect several others at once. Calcium chloride, for example, adds BOTH calcium and chloride at the same time, gypsum adds BOTH calcium and sulfate at the same time, and acid affects both alkalinity and pH at once.

This is probably the main point of this entire page: the goal is a sensible, balanced overall profile — not "chasing" each individual number in isolation. Notice that the app's own salt suggestion in the "💧 Water Chemistry" module deliberately only solves for chloride and sulfate using calcium chloride and gypsum — it adjusts neither alkalinity/HCO3, magnesium, nor sodium, and clearly flags in the goal-achievement assessment when the target profile is therefore NOT fully reached with exactly the salts chosen. That's not a bug in the app — it's precisely an example of how no single adjustment solves the whole picture by itself.

Measuring pH

pH strips can give a rough indication, but are normally far less precise than an electronic pH meter. An electronic pH meter must be calibrated — the app's own water-measurement equipment checklist mentions calibration with pH 4.01 and 7.00 buffer solution as an example.

The electrode also needs to be maintained and stored correctly between uses, and temperature affects both the measurement itself and the sample's actual pH, see mash pH above. Always follow the manufacturer's own instructions for storage solution/method for your specific electrode — not a general tip about, say, distilled water if that contradicts what the manufacturer actually says. This page recommends no specific brand or pH meter model.

"I Didn't Hit My pH" — Now What?

Didn't hit your target mash pH? Mash pH too HIGH can be caused by high alkalinity in the water, a malt profile with little acid contribution of its own (e.g. a very pale recipe), too little acid added, or the water profile in general. Mash pH too LOW can be caused by a lot of dark/roasted malt, too much acid added, or very low alkalinity in the source water.

Don't just pour in acid or baking soda at random because a number looked wrong. Make changes gradually, re-measure afterward, and document what you actually changed — the app's fields for measured mash pH, measurement time, and salt/acid choices exist precisely to support this kind of documentation over time.

Practical Checklist

Three short checklists covering the whole chain, from planning to assessment after the brew:

Before brewing:

  • Do you know your source water?
  • Is alkalinity/bicarbonate known — or deliberately registered as unknown?
  • What malt profile does the recipe have (pale/caramel/dark)?
  • What's the desired ion profile for this particular style?
  • Do you need acid in the mash?
  • Should the sparge water be treated?

During the mash:

  • Measure pH in a consistent way (same timing, same sample handling)
  • Note the time, temperature and sample method
  • Make small adjustments, not large spontaneous doses

After brewing:

  • Log the actual measured pH
  • Log which salts and acids you actually used, and the amounts
  • Compare with flavour and yield the next time you brew the same or a similar recipe

Water chemistry is rarely the first place a beginner mistake happens, but it's often the place where an already-good brew becomes even better. Start simple: know your source water, measure mash pH consistently, and make small, documented adjustments — overall balance usually beats chasing individual numbers.
