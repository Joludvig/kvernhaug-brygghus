A reference for anyone building a recipe in Kvernhaug Brygghus. Everything here is written to answer two questions: what does this mean, and why should I care? Use the contents menu to jump around, or scroll down like an ordinary handbook.

A. Getting Started

Build Your First Beer Recipe

Start in Basics with a beer name and batch volume (how many litres you're brewing). Then add at least one malt under Malt — the search field filters as you type. Do the same under Hops and pick a Yeast. The numbers in Results (OG/FG/ABV/IBU/EBC) and the flavor wheel update automatically with every choice — there's no "calculate" button to press.

Why this matters: There's no "wrong" starting point. Experiment, watch how the numbers change, and adjust until they match the style you're going for — or just whatever sounds good to you.

Brewing Apprentice and Brewing Master

The two buttons at the top switch between two views of the same recipe. Brewing Apprentice hides technical detail and shows more guidance — good when you're new. Brewing Master shows everything: BU:GU, nearby styles, full style analysis. No data disappears when you switch — only how much shows on screen changes.

Why this matters: You can switch back and forth as often as you like, at any point in the process. Many people start in Brewing Apprentice and move to Brewing Master as the terms become familiar.

Units: Metric or US customary

The menu (☰) has a small «Units» switch — choose between Metric (liters, kg, grams) and US customary (US gallons, pounds, ounces). The choice is independent of language: you can read the site in Norwegian and still see metric numbers, or read it in English with US units. The recipe is always stored the same way internally no matter what you choose to see — switching units only changes the display, never the recipe itself.

This lets brewers who are used to different measurement systems share the same saved/exported recipe and see the numbers in whichever unit they know best, without anything being converted incorrectly or lost along the way.

Scaling the recipe (batch size)

"📐 Scale recipe" (in Brewing Master) changes the whole recipe from one batch volume to another, without you having to recalculate every ingredient by hand. Batch volume is how many litres of finished beer you're aiming for — the field you fill in right at the start, under Basics. Enter the new volume you want in "Scale to" and click the button: all malt (kg) and all hops (grams) change proportionally, using the same factor. Going from, say, 23 L to 30 L scales everything by a factor of 30 ÷ 23 ≈ 1.30 — 5 kg of malt becomes about 6.52 kg, 25 g of hops becomes about 32.5 g.

Why this matters: Because ALL malt and hop amounts change by the same factor, the recipe keeps the same ratios between ingredients — and therefore the same character and style profile — just at a different batch size. That's the difference from just editing individual amounts by hand, which easily shifts the balance. Beer name, brewer, brewery, notes, chosen style, yeast choice, hop time, and any custom alpha acid override are not changed by scaling, and scaling itself only happens when you click the button — never automatically while you type in the volume field.

Malt: kg and % are linked — but not the same way both directions

Every malt row in Brewing Master has both a kg field and a % field. The two behave deliberately differently: change kg, and every % field updates automatically and instantly from the new total — kg is always the source of truth. Type directly in the % field, though, and the opposite happens: the kg values don't change while you type, and the other % fields aren't rewritten automatically either. The percentage total above the malt list still updates live, so you can see whether you've reached 100%.

Why this matters: This gives you room to set up a whole percentage split — say, 70% base malt, 20% caramel malt, 10% smoked malt — before anything actually changes the kg amounts. If kg updated on every keystroke in a % field, earlier rows would keep shifting under you while you were still working out the split. Once the percentages are how you want them, make them take effect with "Apply percentage split" (see below).

"Apply percentage split"

This button takes the percentage values you've typed and applies them to the actual kg amounts. Rows where you've typed a % value are treated as "locked" and keep exactly the percentage you set. The remaining (untouched) rows split what's left between themselves proportionally, so the total always comes out to exactly 100%. Total malt weight — the sum of all kg before you clicked the button — stays the same; only the split between rows changes.

Example: With 5 kg total split across two malts, type 70% in one row and click the button — the other row automatically gets the remaining 30%, and the kg fields update to match (3.5 kg / 1.5 kg). If you haven't typed into any % field yet, the button does nothing — it has nothing "locked" to split the rest around.

Target IBU: bitterness worked backwards

Normally the builder works bitterness out forwards: you enter grams, boil time, and alpha acid for a hop addition, and the IBU contribution is calculated automatically. "Target IBU (this addition)" reverses that: you enter how many IBU you want this specific addition to contribute, then click "Calculate grams" — the builder works out how many grams that takes, given boil time, alpha acid, batch volume, and expected OG. This is the inverse Tinseth calculation, and the field only appears for additions with a boil time above 0 minutes (dry hops have no boil utilisation to calculate).

Why this matters: Say "I want this addition to contribute about 15 IBU" — "Calculate grams" fills in the gram weight that gives roughly that contribution, using the same formula as the rest of the builder, just backwards. Target IBU isn't a live link; the calculation only runs when you click the button, just like scaling and "Apply percentage split". Keep in mind this is a calculated starting point, not a guarantee — actual bitterness in the finished beer is also shaped by your kettle, boil-off, wort clarity, and the rest of your own brewing process.

Save a Recipe

"💾 Save recipe" saves the recipe in this browser (localStorage) under the name you gave it. It shows up under "My saved recipes" further down, and you can reload it any time — even after closing the tab.

Why this matters: The recipe is stored locally in this browser, so it only exists on this device. To back it up or move it to another computer or phone, use "📄 Save recipe file (.kbhrecipe)" instead — that's the normal, portable way to back up and share a recipe. Raw JSON export is still available under "Advanced" for legacy files or further processing, but .kbhrecipe is the recommended format for everyday use.

Open or Import a Recipe

Click "Open in builder" on a saved recipe under "My recipes" to load it instantly. Have a .kbhrecipe file (or an older, raw recipe JSON)? Use "📂 Open recipe file" in the builder, or the "Open file" option on the "📥 Import Recipe" page — both open it directly, no re-typing needed. Have a recipe as plain pasted text instead (e.g. "5 kg Maris Otter", "20 g Magnum 60 min")? Use "📥 Import Recipe" and its "Paste text" mode — custom ingredients included.

Printing

Under "Print" you'll find four separate sheets: Recipe sheet (for sharing the recipe with others), Shopping list (ingredients only), Brew day sheet (a worksheet for the brew day itself), and Brew log (a blank form for actual results, to fill in by hand). Each sheet is its own clean page — not just a screenshot printout.

B. Understanding the Recipe

OG — Original Gravity

OG measures the amount of sugar dissolved in the wort before fermentation starts — typically a number like 1.050. The higher the OG, the more sugar the yeast has to work with.

Why this matters: OG is the starting point for both alcohol strength and body. Increase the malt amount (or your brewhouse efficiency) and OG rises. Reduce malt or add more water and it drops. A light, easy-drinking beer typically sits around 1.035–1.045; a strong beer can go above 1.070.

FG — Final Gravity

FG measures the sugar left over once fermentation is finished. The difference between OG and FG tells you how much sugar was actually converted to alcohol.

Why this matters: A low FG gives a drier beer, a high FG gives more residual sweetness and mouthfeel. In this recipe builder, FG is driven by the yeast's expected attenuation — pick a yeast with higher attenuation and FG drops. The actual FG you measure after brewing can differ slightly from the calculated number — see the FAQ.

ABV — Alcohol Strength

ABV (Alcohol By Volume) is the alcohol percentage in the finished beer, calculated from the difference between OG and FG.

Why this matters: To get a stronger beer, either OG needs to go up (more malt) or FG needs to come down (a higher-attenuation yeast) — or both. A session beer usually sits under 4.5%, a typical pale ale around 5–6%, an imperial stout can pass 9–10%.

IBU — Bitterness

IBU (International Bitterness Units) measures hop bitterness. Higher numbers mean a more bitter beer. Most beer styles fall somewhere between 10 and 70 IBU.

Why this matters: IBU is driven by hop amount, alpha acid, and boil time — see alpha acid. Hops added early in the boil (60 min) give the most bitterness; hops added late (0–10 min) give more aroma and flavor but little bitterness. Double the amount of an early hop addition and IBU rises sharply; move that same hop to the end of the boil and IBU drops sharply even though the amount is the same.

EBC — Color

EBC measures the beer's color, from pale gold (low numbers, under 10) through copper and amber to almost black (over 80).

Why this matters: Color comes almost entirely from the malt — darker malt (like roasted malt or crystal malt) gives a far bigger EBC increase per kilo than pale base malt. Add just 200 g of roasted malt to an otherwise pale recipe and EBC can jump noticeably even though the amount is small.

BU:GU — Bitterness vs. Gravity

BU:GU (shown in Brewing Master) is the ratio of IBU to OG (scaled), and says something about how much bitterness stands up against the beer's original gravity/malt backbone. A low number means a malt-forward beer, a high number means a hop-forward beer.

Why this matters: Two beers can have the same IBU but taste completely different depending on how strong the wort (OG) was to begin with. BU:GU is a quick check on whether the balance between bitterness and malt backbone matches what you're aiming for. Note that BU:GU uses OG — it isn't a direct measure of the beer's residual sweetness, which is instead governed by FG.

Attenuation

Attenuation is how much of the available sugar the yeast manages to convert to alcohol, given as a percentage. A yeast with 75% attenuation "eats" 75% of the available sugar.

Why this matters: Higher attenuation gives a lower FG and therefore a drier beer with slightly higher ABV, at the same OG. Pick a library yeast and attenuation fills in automatically — you can override it manually if you're using a custom yeast or know something specific about your particular strain.

Alpha Acid

Alpha acid (α-acid) is the percentage of bittering compounds in the hop. Higher alpha acid means more bitterness per gram of hops.

Why this matters: The alpha acid content of a given hop variety normally varies somewhat from season to season and pack to pack — what's actually printed on your pack can differ from the library's typical value. Feel free to adjust the alpha field to match what's actually printed on your pack for a more precise IBU estimate; see the FAQ.

Brewhouse Efficiency

Efficiency is how much of the malt's sugar potential you actually get into the wort with your own equipment and technique — 75% is a common starting point, but it varies from brewery to brewery.

Why this matters: Efficiency feeds directly into the OG calculation. Brewing Apprentice hides the field and uses 75% as an estimate, so you can get started without needing to know this first. In Brewing Master you can adjust it based on your own measured brews — see also the FAQ.

Malt Potential

Malt potential is how much sugar a given malt can contribute per kilo — the basis for the OG calculation. Different malt types have different potential; a pure base malt gives more than a specialty malt with lower fermentable content.

Why this matters: If you enter a custom malt, you need to supply an estimated potential yourself (the manufacturer's data sheet, if you have it, is the best source). Too high a number overestimates OG, too low underestimates it.

The Flavor Wheel

The flavor wheel is a radar chart with 18 flavor axes, based on your malt, hop, and yeast choices. The further an axis stretches toward the edge, the more prominent that flavor is expected to be.

Why this matters: It's a quick, visual check on whether the recipe is actually pointing in the direction you intended — for example, whether a "citrus/tropical" axis really stands out when you've used plenty of Citra and Mosaic. The chart is an expectation based on the ingredient data, not a guarantee of how the actual beer will taste.

Style Matching

Style matching compares the recipe's numbers (OG/FG/ABV/IBU/EBC) and flavor profile against known beer styles in Kvernhaug Brygghus's own library, and shows which style the recipe resembles most — plus a few nearby alternatives.

Why this matters: It's a guideline, not a verdict. See style ranges and the FAQ for how to interpret it. The library covers 26 styles — this is not the entire official BJCP 2021 style guide (which has around 100 sub-styles).

Style Ranges

Each style in the library has typical ranges for OG/FG/IBU/EBC/ABV, drawn from well-known style descriptions. When your recipe deviates from a selected style's typical range, the style guidance explains this in calm language — "slightly outside" or "clearly outside" — never as an error message.

Why this matters: Style ranges describe what's typical for a style, not a rule you must follow. Many good beers sit deliberately outside a style's typical range.

C. Ingredients

Malt

Malt is the grain that gives the wort sugar (for alcohol), color, and much of the base flavor. It's the source of nearly all the OG and EBC in the recipe.

Hops

Hops provide bitterness (early in the boil) and aroma/flavor (late in the boil or after boiling). The same hop variety can therefore contribute completely differently depending on when it's added. See the full guide to hops in depth for whirlpool/hopstand, dry hop, hop creep and troubleshooting.

Yeast

Yeast converts sugar into alcohol and CO₂, but also significantly affects flavor and mouthfeel — see how yeast affects the beer below.

Base Malt vs. Specialty Malt

Base malt (e.g. Pilsner or Pale Ale malt) normally makes up the bulk of the grain bill — pale color, high potential, neutral-to-mild flavor. Specialty malt (crystal malt, roasted malt, wheat, oats, and others) is used in smaller amounts to adjust color, body, and flavor.

Why this matters: A recipe with 100% base malt often ends up thin and one-dimensional in flavor; too much specialty malt, on the other hand, can dominate and make the beer's profile muddled. Most successful recipes have base malt as the backbone and specialty malt as seasoning.

Bittering Hops vs. Aroma/Flavor Hops

Some hop varieties have high alpha acid and suit a clean, early bittering addition. Others are chosen for their aroma and are used late in the boil or as a "dry hop" after fermentation, where bitterness barely has time to develop.

Why this matters: The same total gram count of hops can give a completely different result depending on whether it's split early (bitterness) or late (aroma). See IBU for the connection to boil time, and hops in depth for whirlpool/hopstand and dry hop.

How Yeast Affects the Beer

Your yeast choice affects not just attenuation (and therefore FG/ABV), but also flavor compounds the yeast itself produces — esters (fruitiness), phenols (spice/clove in certain wheat and Belgian styles), and how fermentation temperature influences both.

Why this matters: Two identical recipes can taste strikingly different with two different yeast strains — even when OG, FG, and IBU are nearly the same. See yeast selection for a full walkthrough of what the strain actually affects.

Custom Ingredients

Can't find your malt, hop, or yeast in the library? Click "+ Custom" on the row and enter the name and basic technical values yourself (EBC/potential for malt, alpha acid for hops, attenuation for yeast). Custom ingredients work fully in the calculations and travel with your recipe through saving, export, and import.

Why this matters: Custom ingredients are saved only on your recipe — they never end up in the shared library other users see.

Why Alpha % Can Vary from Pack to Pack

The alpha acid content of hops is a natural plant trait that varies with growing season, climate, and harvest — not a fixed number. The library shows a typical value, but the actual pack from the manufacturer can differ somewhat.

Why this matters: If you have the pack in hand, adjust the alpha field on the hop row to match what's actually printed on it — it gives a more precise IBU estimate than the library average.

Why Manufacturer Data Can Vary

EBC, potential, alpha acid, and attenuation are all properties that in practice vary somewhat from batch to batch at the manufacturer — the Kvernhaug Brygghus library shows typical, curated values, not a guarantee for your specific pack or package.

D. Brewing Process — Quick Glossary

This is a quick glossary. For a full step-by-step walkthrough of the brew day itself, see A Brew Day — From Water to Fermenter.

Cleaning and Sanitizing

Two different steps: cleaning removes visible dirt and residue, sanitizing reduces microorganisms on equipment that's already clean. Dirty equipment can't be "saved" just by sanitizing it — dirt shields bacteria from the sanitizer. Anything that touches the wort/beer after the boil must be both clean and sanitized.

Mashing

Mixing crushed malt with hot water at a specific temperature and time, so the malt's enzymes convert starch into fermentable sugar. See also advanced mashing methods in Brewing Master (Hochkurz, decoction, double mashing) for more than a single mash temperature.

Mash-out

A brief heat-up to around 75–78°C after mashing, which stops enzyme activity and makes the wort easier to sparge.

Sparging

Rinsing remaining sugar out of the grain bed with hot water, so as much of the potential as possible ends up in the kettle — not used in every method (see brewing methods).

Pre-Boil Volume and Pre-Boil Gravity

The amount of wort (pre-boil volume) and its gravity (pre-boil gravity/SG) right before you start the boil timer — both measured before any water has evaporated through boiling. An early checkpoint against the plan: if the numbers deviate a lot from what you'd planned, it can indicate a mash or sparge efficiency different from expected, long before you reach the OG reading after the boil. Water keeps evaporating throughout the boil itself (see boil), so it's normal for pre-boil gravity to be lower than OG — that's not a deviation in itself.

Boil

The wort is boiled, normally for 60 minutes, to sterilize, drive off unwanted compounds, and let the hops release bitterness and aroma. Water evaporates during the boil while the sugar stays behind, so the wort becomes more concentrated and OG rises — how much actually evaporates ("boil-off") varies with equipment and boil intensity.

Hop Additions

Hops added at specific points in the boil — early for bitterness, late for aroma. See IBU.

Chilling

Rapidly cooling the wort after the boil, down toward the yeast's starting temperature — the faster, the lower the risk of unwanted bacterial growth and the clearer the beer.

OG Reading

Measuring the wort's gravity right before fermentation, with a hydrometer or refractometer — compared against the calculated OG.

Hydrometer — Temperature Correction

A hydrometer is calibrated for one specific temperature, normally printed on the instrument itself — 20 °C is common, but the instrument decides, not a universal rule. Measuring a sample that deviates significantly from that calibration temperature can give a misleading reading. Check what's actually printed on your own hydrometer, and correct the reading (or cool/warm the sample to the reference temperature) for large deviations.

Fermentation

The period during which yeast converts sugar into alcohol and CO₂ — normally lasts 1–3 weeks depending on yeast type, strength, and temperature.

Diacetyl and Diacetyl Rest

Diacetyl is a fermentation byproduct that, in excess, can give a buttery or butterscotch-like impression. Yeast normally cleans up much of this itself toward the end of fermentation, given enough time at the right temperature — "diacetyl rest" is a term used especially in lager brewing, but the principle applies more broadly: don't cool or package the beer too early. There's no single universal temperature or schedule that fits every yeast strain and fermentation course.

Oxidation After Fermentation

A bit of oxygen is wanted BEFORE pitching the yeast (see transferring to the fermenter) — it helps the yeast get going. Oxygen reaching the beer AFTER fermentation is actively underway is something else entirely: it can cause oxidation, which can dull fresh hop aroma, produce darker/cardboard-like/"stale" notes, and reduce shelf life. Avoid unnecessary splashing during transfers, and use closed transfers where practical — but a single, stray oxygen molecule won't automatically ruin the whole batch.

FG Reading

Measuring the gravity once fermentation has settled down, to confirm it's finished and calculate the actual ABV. A refractometer works directly on unfermented wort, but gives a misleading reading as soon as alcohol is present in the sample — use an alcohol/refractometer correction, or measure with a hydrometer instead.

Refractometer and Brix

Many refractometers show Brix instead of SG. Before fermentation, Brix can be converted to an approximate SG value without issue. Once fermentation has started, that simple conversion no longer holds — alcohol in the sample bends light differently than sugar, so a correct calculation must then use both the original wort strength and the current Brix reading together, see FG reading. If you're unsure whether the correction is accurate, a hydrometer is often the simplest check measurement.

Cold Crash

Chilling the beer further down once fermentation is actually finished (see fermentation seems stopped) — common examples fall somewhere around 0–4°C for a day to a few days, but this varies with equipment and desired result, and there's no single fixed number. Lower temperature makes yeast, proteins, and other particles settle out faster, often giving a clearer beer. The rapid temperature change can create a vacuum in a closed fermenter, which can in turn draw air/oxygen in if the vessel isn't sealed or handled properly, see oxidation after fermentation. See clarity for what cold crashing actually works on — and what it does not fix.

Carbonation

Adding CO₂ to the finished beer — either naturally (sugar + residual yeast in bottle/keg) or by force-carbonating with CO₂ pressure in a keg.

Bottling/Kegging, in General

The beer is transferred to bottles or a keg for storage, conditioning, and serving. The choice mainly affects how carbonation is handled.

Pressure Fermentation (Safety)

Only use a fermenter that's manufacturer-rated for pressure, with a working spunding/pressure relief valve set up per the manufacturer's specifications. Never exceed the equipment's stated maximum working pressure, and never block a safety valve. Always follow the manufacturer's own instructions — equipment varies. See the full guide to pressure fermentation, spunding and closed transfer for the practical explanation. For general brewing safety beyond pressure (hot wort, steam, chemicals, glass, CO2 cylinders), see general safety in the brew day guide.

E. Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers to individual questions. For a more systematic walkthrough of taste, smell and common off-flavours — including a practical troubleshooting flow — see sensorics and troubleshooting.

Do all values need to fall within the beer style?

No. Style ranges are typical ranges, not rules. Many good, deliberate beers fall outside on one or more points.

Is the recipe wrong if one value is outside range?

No — the style guidance flags it ("slightly outside" or "clearly outside"), but that's information, not an error message. Use it to judge whether the deviation is intentional.

Why does IBU change when I change the alpha acid?

IBU is calculated directly from the hop's alpha acid content, amount, and boil time. Higher alpha acid means more bittering compound per gram of hops, and therefore higher IBU at the same amount and time. See IBU and alpha acid.

Why does the beer get darker when I add this malt?

Malt's color contribution varies enormously — a dark, roasted malt can have 10–50 times higher EBC than a pale base malt. Even a small amount of a dark malt can therefore have a large effect on the beer's overall color. See EBC.

What happens if I switch yeast?

Attenuation (and therefore FG and ABV) changes to the new yeast's value, and the flavor profile can change — different yeast strains contribute different esters and phenols. See how yeast affects the beer.

What does attenuation mean?

See attenuation above — in short, how much of the sugar the yeast manages to convert to alcohol.

Can I use ingredients that aren't in the library?

Yes — use "+ Custom" on the malt, hop, or yeast row. See custom ingredients.

Why didn't I hit my expected OG on brew day?

The calculated OG assumes your brewhouse efficiency (the field in Basics) actually matches your equipment and technique that day. Crush quality, mash temperature, sparging method, and even weather conditions can cause deviation. Feel free to adjust the efficiency number after a few of your own brews, so future estimates become more precise for your specific setup.

What's the difference between OG and FG?

OG is measured before fermentation (sugar present), FG is measured after (sugar remaining). See OG and FG.

Why is the actual FG different from the calculated FG?

Calculated FG uses the yeast's typical attenuation percentage. Actual attenuation is affected by fermentation temperature, yeast amount/health, mash temperature (which governs how fermentable the wort actually is), and the yeast strain's variation from batch to batch. A small deviation is normal and no cause for concern.

Fermentation seems to have stopped — what do I do?

Don't rely solely on the airlock having stopped bubbling — that can just as easily mean a poor seal as a finished fermentation. Measure SG (see FG reading), check that the temperature is within the yeast's recommended range, and compare several readings over a couple of days. Only bottle or keg once FG is stable.

The beer tastes too sweet / FG is higher than expected — what does that mean?

First check that FG is actually stable (see above) — fermentation may simply not be finished yet. If FG is stable and still high, the cause could be the yeast's actual attenuation, fermentation temperature, or a mash temperature that produced a less fermentable wort than planned. Adding more hops doesn't "fix" residual sweetness — it changes the bitterness, not the sugar content.

I suspect an infection in the beer — what now?

Unusual smell, taste, or appearance (e.g. a film on the surface, unexpected sourness) CAN be caused by infection, but can also have other explanations — don't conclude from a single observation alone. Good hygiene throughout the process (see cleaning and sanitizing) is the most important prevention.

Too little or too much carbonation — what do I do?

Don't blindly add extra sugar to "fix" low carbonation — first check that FG was actually stable before bottling (see above), since a fermentation that wasn't finished can continue in the bottle. Too much carbonation isn't just a taste issue: overcarbonation can cause dangerous overpressure, see the safety warning in the brew day guide.

Why was my efficiency/OG lower than expected?

Low efficiency is rarely a single mistake — it's usually the result of the whole mash and sparge process taken together. Common contributors: crush consistency (too coarse or too fine), mash temperature, mash time, water-to-grain ratio, poor circulation in the mash, sparging method and technique, an inaccurate measured volume, or an inaccurate temperature/gravity reading. See also why didn't I hit my expected OG.

Why was my OG higher than expected?

Common causes are often the opposite of low efficiency: less final volume than planned (for example more boil-off than expected, see the boil), higher brewhouse efficiency than assumed that day, or an error in the volume or gravity measurement. A high OG isn't necessarily a problem in itself, but it affects your expected FG and ABV.

My beer smells of sulfur during fermentation — is that wrong?

Certain yeast strains produce sulfur compounds as a perfectly normal part of fermentation, and the smell often fades with time and a bit of conditioning. It isn't automatically a sign of infection. If a strong sulfur smell persists well after fermentation and a few weeks of conditioning, it may be worth reconsidering process and hygiene.

My beer tastes hot/solvent-like — what could cause that?

Common contributors are a high fermentation temperature, stressed yeast (for example too little yeast pitched), a high OG giving the yeast a heavier job to do, or insufficient oxygenation/pitch rate at the start — see yeast health and temperature. Note that not all noticeable alcohol heat in strong beers means something is wrong — it can mellow with a few weeks or months of conditioning, see conditioning and storage.

My beer tastes buttery/butterscotch — is that diacetyl?

A buttery or butterscotch character often points to diacetyl, see diacetyl and the diacetyl rest. First consider whether fermentation actually got enough time at the right temperature toward the end (a diacetyl rest) before concluding something is wrong — this is often something the yeast cleans up on its own, given enough time.

My beer tastes papery/stale — could that be oxidation?

Papery or "stale" notes, muted hop aroma, and sometimes a slightly darker color than expected can point to oxidation, see oxidation after fermentation. Consider whether the beer may have gotten unnecessary air contact after fermentation was actively underway — splashing during transfers, a poorly sealed cold crash, or long storage at a high temperature are common ways it happens.

When should I NOT worry?

Some observations look dramatic but are often perfectly normal: an airlock going quiet doesn't automatically mean fermentation has stopped (see fermentation seems to have stopped); krausen can drop early without anything being wrong; a sulfur smell can be normal for certain yeast strains (see above); a hazy beer doesn't necessarily mean infection; and strong alcohol heat in strong beers can mellow with a bit of time. Hydrometer/FG readings are a more reliable indicator than airlock activity alone — learn to observe and measure before you "fix".

F. Water Chemistry — Brief Introduction

Kvernhaug Brygghus has a dedicated "💧 Water Chemistry" module in the recipe builder (Brewmaster) where you can register your source water, set a target profile, and get salt suggestions. This section only explains why these numbers matter — not how to use the module itself step by step. For alkalinity, mash pH, acid additions, sparge water and other Master Brewer topics, see advanced water chemistry.

pH

Mash pH affects how well the enzymes in the mash work, and therefore how efficiently starch is converted into fermentable sugar.

<strong>Why you care:</strong> A common target range for mash pH is a guiding direction to aim for, not a magic exact number you must hit to the decimal.

Ca — Calcium

Calcium is arguably the most important single ion in brewing water — it's involved in the brewing process itself, not just in flavor.

<strong>Why you care:</strong> Calcium can, among other things, contribute to better yeast health/flocculation, and affects water chemistry (including mash pH) indirectly.

Mg — Magnesium

Magnesium is a nutrient the yeast needs, but only in small amounts.

<strong>Why you care:</strong> Malt itself normally provides enough magnesium for the yeast. High levels in the water can instead give an unwanted flavor — more isn't better here.

Na — Sodium

Sodium in moderate amounts can contribute to a rounder, fuller impression in the beer.

<strong>Why you care:</strong> Too much sodium can instead give a salty or harsh flavor — as with most ions here, it's about a sensible range, not "more is better".

Cl — Chloride

Chloride is often associated with a rounder, fuller malt/mouthfeel.

<strong>Why you care:</strong> This is a general tendency, not a rule — the actual concentration, not just the ratio to sulfate, also matters for the result.

SO4 — Sulfate

Sulfate is often associated with a drier, sharper emphasis on hop bitterness.

<strong>Why you care:</strong> As with chloride, this is a tendency, not a rule — very high sulfate levels can themselves come across as a plain, mineral sharpness.

Cl:SO4 Ratio

The ratio between chloride and sulfate (Cl:SO4) gives a rough signal of which direction a water profile leans — more toward malt/fullness, or more toward a dry, sharp hop emphasis.

Why you care: An important nuance: the ratio alone does not determine the flavor, and it isn't a universal "malt vs. hops" switch. The actual ion concentrations — not just the ratio between them — also matter a great deal. Two water profiles with the same Cl:SO4 ratio but very different absolute levels can taste quite different.

G. Yeast, Carbonation & Conditioning

Yeast Starter

A yeast starter is a small amount of sterile wort you pitch yeast into ahead of time, let multiply for anywhere from a few hours to a couple of days, and use as a "boosted" yeast package on brew day itself. Especially relevant for liquid yeast, low cell counts in the package, or ahead of a strong wort that needs a lot of yeast. The goal is primarily more, healthier yeast cells — not necessarily a whole separate process. Hygienic handling throughout is critical, since the starter is an environment where unwanted microorganisms can multiply just as readily as the yeast itself.

Why you care: A yeast starter isn't a universal necessity for all yeast — many brews do fine without one. Dry yeast is often handled differently (rehydrated or pitched directly, see pitch yeast), and the manufacturer's own recommendations should be followed where they exist. A starter is also useful for waking up and assessing yeast harvested from an earlier brew, see starter from stored yeast.

Yeast Health and Temperature

Too little yeast relative to the wort's size and strength can cause a slow or stressed fermentation. Temperature also plays a major role: a temperature too high or too low for what the yeast strain is built for can affect both how well the yeast works and which flavor compounds it produces along the way. Different yeast strains have different working ranges, and a sharp or sudden temperature rise can create off-flavors that wouldn't otherwise occur. A high OG also gives the yeast a heavier job to do.

Why you care: The manufacturer's stated temperature range is a sensible starting point — not proof that exactly one temperature suits every beer or every yeast strain. See also the fermentation step in a brew day.

Natural vs. Forced Carbonation

Natural carbonation: sugar is added after fermentation is finished, and residual yeast produces CO₂ in a sealed container — most common when bottling, but can also be used in a keg. Forced carbonation: CO₂ is supplied directly from a bottle or tank — most common in a keg. With forced carbonation, pressure and temperature determine how much CO₂ actually dissolves into the beer: colder beer holds more CO₂, and higher pressure gives more dissolved CO₂ at a given temperature.

Why you care: Desired carbonation level varies by beer style — there's no single universal psi or bar value that suits every beer. See priming/bottle carbonation for the practical side of natural carbonation.

Priming / Bottle Carbonation

With bottle carbonation ("priming"), a calculated amount of sugar is added right before bottling, so that residual yeast produces CO₂ inside the sealed bottle. FG must be stable before doing this (see fermentation seems to have stopped) — the beer may already contain some dissolved CO₂ from fermentation itself, and a correctly calculated priming amount should account for that. The sugar amount should be calculated from the desired carbonation level, not eyeballed.

Why you care: Too much sugar combined with residual fermentation activity can cause dangerous overpressure in the bottle, see the safety warning in the brew day guide. Too little sugar instead gives a flatter beer than planned.

Conditioning and Storage

Beer can keep changing after packaging. Some beers — especially pale, hop-forward styles — are usually best fresh, since hop aroma often fades with time. Others — especially stronger or darker beers — can keep developing and sometimes benefit from longer conditioning. Oxidation (see oxidation after fermentation) happens faster at higher temperatures, so a dark, cool storage spot is generally preferable to a warm, bright one.

Why you care: How well a beer suits aging is style- and recipe-dependent — "older is always better" doesn't hold as a general rule. Serve fresh beers within a reasonable time, and feel free to give strong/dark beers a bit more time if you're curious how they develop.
